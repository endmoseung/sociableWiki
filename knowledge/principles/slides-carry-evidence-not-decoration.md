# In a Technical Talk, the Screen Carries a Task or Evidence — Not Decoration

A common piece of slide advice is "never put a text-only slide up; always add an image". I think it points at the right problem with the wrong rule. Good talks don't put an image on every slide. What they do is change **the thing the audience has to think about** each time the screen changes. The image is only worth adding when it does that job.

## The decision rule

Pick the screen by asking what the claim on that slide needs:

- **If the claim is a change**, use animation — show the before turning into the after.
- **If the claim is that something actually works**, show a demo or a short video.
- **If the claim is a comparison**, put the conditions and the relevant values on the same screen.
- **If the moment is a judgment, an admission of failure, or a conclusion**, leave one sentence — or an empty screen.
- **If there is no new information and no shift of attention**, don't add a slide at all.

TED's own speaker guidance doesn't treat slides as required. When you do use them, it recommends one point per slide and letting images and data do the explaining. So the more accurate rule is not "no text-only slides", but **"don't attach a decorative image to a claim that needs evidence."**

## What the well-known speakers actually do

Looking at talks people cite as models, the pattern is about matching the screen to the claim, not about image density:

- **Steve Jobs** alternated between competitor comparisons, live demos, per-user numbers, and the physical reveal. The iPod's "1,000 songs in your pocket" line came *after* the screen had already shown storage, transfer time, battery life, and size.
- **Hans Rosling** used animated data as evidence of change over time, not as decoration. He explained the axes and the legend first, then pressed play.
- **David Gallo** cut his narration down when the footage itself was the claim.
- **Ken Robinson** and **Brené Brown** barely used the screen during stretches built on story and judgment.
- **Nancy Duarte's** sparkline is a rhetorical structure that moves between "what is" and "what could be". It is not a fixed formula for slide transitions.
- **Garr Reynolds** argues for dropping slides that duplicate the script — but not for dropping quantitative evidence.
- **Edward Tufte** stresses putting the values a judgment depends on within a single field of view.

## What the controlled evidence supports — and how far

Garner and Alley's assertion–evidence study showed 110 engineering undergraduates an eight-minute recorded presentation. The version that combined a full-sentence assertion headline, explanatory visual evidence, little body text, and step-by-step reveal did better on understanding the relationships in an unfamiliar technical process, and on recall ten days later. On immediate multiple-choice factual recall overall, there was no clear difference.

That study changed several elements at once, and it did not test live conference talks to expert audiences. So I only stretch it this far: **for an unfamiliar causal process, pairing the claim with direct evidence probably helps understanding.** It is not evidence that every slide needs an image.

## A checklist for building a technical talk

1. Is the slide title the claim this slide will prove?
2. Does the screen — the UI, the code, the number — directly support that claim?
3. Can the audience see the before/after, cause/effect, or comparison within one view?
4. Am I repeating on the slide the sentence I'm about to say out loud? If so, cut it.
5. Is there exactly one judgment the audience has to make on this slide?
6. Does each number play only one role — scope, problem, cause, or result?
7. Any number I couldn't trace to its original source comes out of the talk's headline evidence.

## When this is wrong

If the audience will examine the material closely, separately from the talk, low-density slides alone aren't enough. Then you need a companion document alongside the screen: detailed tables, the full code, and the measurement conditions. The talk carries the argument; the handout carries the inspection.
